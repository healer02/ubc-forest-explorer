1. western redcedar Thuja plicata

conifer · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 1053 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Flat, fern-like sprays of tiny overlapping scale leaves; the undersides show whitish bow-tie or butterfly-shaped markings. needs check
  • bark: Reddish-brown to grey bark that is fibrous and strips off in long vertical ribbons. needs check
  • form: Branches droop then curve up at the tips like a J; the leader often droops slightly; the trunk base is flared and buttressed. needs check

Look-alikes

  • yellow-cedar: Yellow-cedar sprays hang limp like a wet dog, feel prickly when stroked backward, and lack the white bow-tie marks underneath. needs check
  • eastern white cedar (Thuja occidentalis): Usually a hedge or small columnar tree; foliage undersides are yellow-green with little or no white marking. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
A good shade-tolerant screen and character tree in moist sites; a poor choice for hot, paved, drought-prone spots where it struggles.
Ecology needs check
Its wood contains natural fungicidal compounds (thujaplicins) that make it highly rot-resistant.
Controversy needs check
Summer droughts and heat (e.g., the 2021 heat dome) have caused visible dieback and dead tops in redcedar across the region. Should we keep planting it in urban sites as the climate warms?
Tour talking point needs check
BC's official tree and by far the most common tree on campus (about 1 in 8 identified trees, above the 10% single-species guideline often used for urban forests). A cultural keystone for Coast Salish peoples, including the Musqueam, on whose land UBC stands: bark, wood and roots for canoes, houses, clothing and baskets.

2. Douglas-fir Pseudotsuga menziesii

conifer · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 566 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • fruit: Hanging cones with three-pointed bracts sticking out between the scales, often described as the hind legs and tail of a mouse hiding in the cone. needs check
  • leaf: Soft, flat needles 2-3 cm long arranged all around the twig (bottle-brush), with sharp-pointed, shiny reddish-brown buds. needs check
  • bark: Mature bark is very thick, corky and deeply furrowed; young bark is smooth and grey with resin blisters. needs check

Look-alikes

  • grand fir: Grand fir needles lie flat in two rows with notched tips and white stripes underneath; its cones stand upright and fall apart on the tree, so you rarely find whole cones on the ground. needs check
  • western hemlock: Hemlock needles are short and of uneven length, its cones are tiny (about 2 cm), and the very top of the tree nods over. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
A forest-grown tree left standing alone after its neighbours are cleared is more exposed to wind than it grew up with; retain groves rather than single trees where possible.
Ecology needs check
Thick bark lets mature trees survive low-intensity fire; it is a pioneer that regenerates well after disturbance in the drier coastal forests.
Controversy needs check
How much of BC's remaining coastal old-growth should be logged and how much protected is a long-running debate, highlighted by the 2020 Old Growth Strategic Review and the Fairy Creek blockades on Vancouver Island.
Tour talking point needs check
Not a true fir, hence the hyphen: the genus name Pseudotsuga means 'false hemlock'. The species name honours Archibald Menzies; the common name honours David Douglas.

3. western hemlock Tsuga heterophylla

conifer · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 103 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • form: The leader (very top) droops or nods over, and branch tips are soft and drooping, giving a feathery look. needs check
  • leaf: Short, flat needles of mixed lengths (hence 'heterophylla', different leaves) with two whitish bands underneath, in flattened sprays. needs check
  • fruit: Small cones about 2-3 cm long hanging from the branch tips. needs check

Look-alikes

  • Douglas-fir: Douglas-fir has longer, even needles all around the twig, pointed buds, an upright leader and larger cones with three-pointed bracts. needs check
  • western redcedar: Redcedar also has a slightly drooping leader, but its foliage is scale leaves, not needles. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
Thrives in shade beneath other conifers; a poor choice for hot, dry, exposed sites.
Ecology needs check
Very shade tolerant; seedlings often establish on rotting logs and stumps (nurse logs).
Controversy left blank
No confident fact, so left empty.
Tour talking point needs check
Look for the nodding top: a quick way to pick hemlock out of a skyline of conifers. Washington's state tree.

5. yellow-cedar Callitropsis nootkatensis

conifer · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 27 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Scale leaves in limp, hanging sprays that feel prickly when stroked backward; no white markings underneath. needs check
  • fruit: Small round cones about 1 cm across with 4-6 scales, each with a pointed knob; they take two years to mature. needs check
  • form: Branchlets hang vertically, giving a shaggy, weeping look; the leader droops. needs check

Look-alikes

  • western redcedar: Redcedar sprays are flatter and smooth to the touch, show white bow-tie marks underneath, and have small egg-shaped cones with overlapping scales. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
The weeping form ('Pendula') is a popular ornamental; the inventory lists several 'weeping Alaskan yellow-cedar' on campus.
Ecology needs check
Yellow-cedar decline: widespread dieback along the coast of Alaska and northern BC has been linked to reduced snowpack, which leaves shallow roots exposed to freezing.
Controversy needs check
Its scientific name keeps changing (Chamaecyparis, Xanthocyparis, Callitropsis, Cupressus nootkatensis) and botanists still disagree on which genus is right.
Tour talking point needs check
Hands-on test: stroke the foliage backward. Redcedar feels smooth, yellow-cedar feels prickly. Then ask the group which genus name they would put on the sign.

6. vine maple Acer circinatum

broadleaf · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 417 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Nearly round leaves with 7-9 shallow, toothed lobes, arranged opposite. needs check
  • fruit: Paired winged seeds (samaras) spread almost straight out to each side, often red-tinged in summer. needs check
  • form: Multi-stemmed small tree or large shrub with crooked, sprawling stems that can root where they touch the ground. needs check

Look-alikes

  • Japanese maple: Japanese maple leaves are deeply cut into 5-9 narrow, pointed lobes; vine maple leaves are rounder with shallow lobes. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
An excellent native small tree for shady or tight spaces; fall colour is orange-red in sun and yellow in shade.
Ecology needs check
An understory specialist: in deep shade it grows sprawling and vine-like; in sun it is more compact.
Controversy left blank

The most-planted native broadleaf on campus (about 415 trees). 'Circinatum' refers to the rounded leaves. Ask: why use this instead of a Japanese maple?

7. bigleaf maple Acer macrophyllum

broadleaf · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 75 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Very large opposite leaves, often 15-30 cm across, with 5 deep lobes. needs check
  • fruit: Paired samaras with hairy seed cases, joined in a V and hanging in clusters. needs check
  • bark: Grey-brown, furrowed bark that is often thickly covered with mosses and licorice ferns. needs check

Look-alikes

  • Norway maple: Norway maple leaves are smaller, the broken leaf stalk oozes milky sap, and its samaras spread almost flat. needs check
  • London plane: London plane leaves are alternate (not opposite) and its bark flakes in camouflage patches. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
A large, fast-growing shade tree best suited to parks and natural areas where it has room.
Ecology needs check
Its bark carries heavy loads of mosses and ferns, and the tree can grow roots from its branches into these canopy soil mats.
Controversy needs check
Bigleaf maple dieback has been reported in the Pacific Northwest over the past decade, with drought and heat suspected as drivers.
Tour talking point needs check
Often said to have the largest leaves of any maple. Its sap can be tapped for syrup.

8. Pacific dogwood Cornus nuttallii

broadleaf · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 71 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • flower: In spring, 4-7 (often 6) large white bracts surround a button of tiny greenish flowers; it sometimes blooms again in late summer. needs check
  • fruit: Clusters of shiny red-orange berry-like fruits in fall. needs check
  • leaf: Opposite oval leaves with veins that curve toward the tip; tear a leaf gently and stretchy threads hold the halves together. needs check

Look-alikes

  • kousa dogwood: Kousa has 4 pointed bracts, blooms about a month later after its leaves are out, and has a single raspberry-like fruit on a long stalk. needs check
  • eastern flowering dogwood (Cornus florida): Usually 4 bracts with notched tips, and a smaller tree. needs check
  • 'Eddie's White Wonder': A hybrid of Pacific and eastern flowering dogwood; intermediate features, so check the label or bract count. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
Hard to establish in urban sites; prefers well-drained soil in partial shade beneath taller trees.
Ecology needs check
Dogwood anthracnose, a fungal disease (Discula destructiva) first noticed in the late 1970s, has killed many Pacific dogwoods.
Controversy needs check
Should campus keep planting the native dogwood despite anthracnose, or switch to disease-resistant kousa and hybrids?
Tour talking point needs check
BC's floral emblem. What look like petals are bracts: count them and compare with a kousa dogwood nearby.

9. red alder Alnus rubra

broadleaf · Native: Native to coastal BC needs check · 35 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Oval, alternate leaves with coarse double teeth; the leaf edge rolls under slightly. needs check
  • bark: Smooth, light grey bark often blotched white with lichens; scraped inner bark turns orange-red. needs check
  • fruit: Small woody cone-like catkins (about 2 cm) that stay on the tree through winter; long male catkins in early spring. needs check

Look-alikes

  • paper birch: Paper birch has white bark that peels in papery strips, and its leaf edges do not roll under. needs check
Design use / misuse needs check
A fast-growing, short-lived pioneer good for quick cover and restoration; its weak wood makes it a poor long-term street tree.
Ecology needs check
Fixes nitrogen through root nodules with Frankia bacteria, enriching soils after disturbance.
Controversy needs check
Long treated as a weed in conifer plantations and cut or sprayed with herbicide; now increasingly valued for soil building and as a hardwood.
Tour talking point needs check
Scratch test: the inner bark turns orange, the 'red' in red alder. Its wood is a traditional choice for smoking salmon.

11. Garry oak Quercus garryana

13. deodar cedar Cedrus deodara

conifer · Non-native: Non-native: western Himalaya needs check · 46 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Needles 2.5-5 cm long in dense tufts on short spur shoots, single on new long shoots. needs check
  • form: Branch tips and the leader droop gracefully; broadly conical crown. needs check
  • fruit: Barrel-shaped cones sit upright on the branches and break apart on the tree. needs check

Look-alikes

  • Atlas cedar: Atlas cedar has shorter needles (under 2.5 cm), often blue-grey, and stiffer, upswept branches. needs check

14. giant sequoia Sequoiadendron giganteum

conifer · Non-native: Non-native: Sierra Nevada, California needs check · 19 trees in the inventory

Key ID features

  • leaf: Awl-shaped, scale-like needles that clasp the twig and overlap all around it, making shoots look like ropes. needs check
  • bark: Very thick, soft, spongy, fibrous red-brown bark: you can press into it without hurting your hand. needs check
  • form: Massive columnar trunk with strong taper and a dense, conical crown; branch tips turn upward. needs check

Look-alikes

  • coast redwood: Coast redwood has flat needles in two rows on most shoots (like a yew), with white bands beneath, and smaller cones (about 2 cm vs 5-7 cm). needs check

Ecology needs check
Fire-adapted: thick bark protects the trunk and heat helps open the cones to release seed.
Controversy needs check

Tour talking point needs check
The largest tree on Earth by volume. Press the spongy bark. Campus's biggest recorded is near the First Nations Garden.

15. dawn redwood Metasequoia glyptostroboides

Key ID features

16. ginkgo Ginkgo biloba

Look-alikes
